How the papers get organised

Three layers. The bottom one is free and true. The top one is cheap and rough. Only the middle one costs real money.

Layer 3  —  Summaries this is the RAPTOR part  ·  solves context length
Group similar papers, write a summary of each group, then summarise the groups. A short tree.
When the AI asks a wide question it reads a few group summaries instead of 300 papers. When it asks a narrow one it drops down to single papers.

Layer 2  —  What each paper claims AI-written  ·  must be traceable
For each paper, small fixed facts: this change → had this effect → under these conditions. Plus the numbers used: setpoints, loads, ranges, units.
Every single one carries the paper it came from and the sentence it came from. If it cannot be traced, it is thrown away.
You already have most of this. PaperCard stores problem, method, metrics, limitations. ResearchFinding already stores change → effect → direction → conditions, with the page number. That is a graph already, just stored as lists.
↑ sits on ↑
Layer 1  —  Who cites whom free  ·  exact  ·  no AI involved
Papers, their references, who cites them, authors, venues, years. Straight from OpenAlex. These are facts, not guesses, and they cost nothing.
This is the web you walk to find papers keyword search misses.
Starting from a survey paper — two wins

1. Its reference list is a hand-picked reading list made by an expert. Far better than a keyword search as a starting point.

2. Surveys have a “future work” section. That is experts writing down the gaps, by hand. Hide it, run your gap finder, then compare. Free marking scheme.

Watch out: a survey only knows the past. You must still follow forward citations to catch everything published since.

Why this pays off later

You said this graph gets reused when building Modelica models and choosing what to test. That only works if we store the numbers now, not just the claims.

So when reading each paper, also pull out things like “chilled water setpoint 6–10 °C”, “IT load 400–600 kW”, with units and source.

Then stage 3 can ask: what ranges does the literature actually use for this? — and get a real answer instead of a guess.

One warning about off-the-shelf GraphRAG. It asks an AI to invent the entities and links itself. That gives you a graph full of edges nobody can check, and your whole project is built on being able to check things. Use the idea from GraphRAG — group the graph and summarise each group — but keep your own fixed fields for what gets extracted, so every link points back at a real sentence in a real paper.
